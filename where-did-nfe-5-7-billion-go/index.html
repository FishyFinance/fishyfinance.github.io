Where Did NFE's $5.7 Billion Go?

On September 11, 2026, New Fortress Energy completed its restructuring. The headline was easy to understand: roughly $5.7 billion of third-party debt had been extinguished, leaving the company with about $700 million of corporate debt.

Then you look at what happened to the claims behind that debt. The first surprise is Brazil: NFE had separated its Brazilian operations into a new entity, BrazilCo, and at closing 100% of BrazilCo's common equity went to the plan creditors.

So the restructuring wasn't just about reducing what NFE owed. It also changed who owned part of the business, and Brazil was only the beginning.

The creditors didn't just get their old claims back

The reorganized CoreCo issued $571.3 million of new senior secured term loans to plan creditors. It also issued 2,454,936 Series A mandatorily convertible preferred shares, with an aggregate liquidation preference of $2.4549 billion, along with 10,608,922 shares of CoreCo common stock, representing 65% of CoreCo's common stock at closing.

The plan creditors had become something else: part lender, part preferred investor, part common shareholder and, through BrazilCo, owners of an entire operating business.

That changes the question. A debt claim has a face value and repayment priority; common equity participates in whatever value remains, while preferred equity sits somewhere between those positions with its own rights and conversion mechanics.

Putting those instruments into one headline recovery number would hide more than it reveals.

Then the company split again

There was another layer to the restructuring. FLNG 2 was placed into a separate financing structure for a separate creditor group, with $400 million of non-recourse term loans and $200 million of preferred equity. That $400 million is project-level, non-recourse financing and sits outside the roughly $700 million of corporate debt described above.

Instead of dividing one pool of securities among creditors, the transaction divided the enterprise itself. BrazilCo became one pool of assets and ownership, the continuing CoreCo business another, while FLNG 2 carried its own financing structure.

Different creditors could therefore emerge with exposure to very different parts of what had once been one NFE group. That is a much bigger change than a lower debt balance.

And NFE was still raising money

While the old capital structure was being dismantled, the new one was also being funded. NFE raised $136.5 million of new financing at closing.

The pro forma disclosure shows the detail underneath that figure: a $36.5 million senior term loan was reduced by a $1.5 million original-issue discount to $35 million of net proceeds, alongside $100 million of junior term loans. The resulting $135 million of cash proceeds was therefore lower than the $136.5 million face amount of the financing.

The company was reducing old obligations, changing ownership, creating new securities and bringing in fresh capital at the same time. This was a reconstruction of the capital structure, not just a reduction in it.

The interesting part is what the debt reduction changed

Before the restructuring, NFE's creditors were primarily looking at claims for repayment. After it, part of that exposure had become ownership, with the plan creditors holding the entire equity of BrazilCo and a majority of the common stock of the reorganized CoreCo at closing, alongside preferred equity and new secured debt.

Existing shareholders, meanwhile, were left with the residual common equity position beneath that new structure. The balance sheet had changed, but so had the map of who was exposed to which assets, and once that happens, the $5.7 billion headline becomes only the first number in the story.

Because a dollar of debt is not the same as a dollar of equity

This is where the restructuring becomes difficult to value. The new securities do not all carry the same economics: the term loan has contractual repayment terms and security, the preferred stake has its own priority and conversion mechanics, the common position participates in the residual value of CoreCo, and the Brazilian equity represents ownership of a separate operating business.

The FLNG 2 financing adds another layer because its claims are tied to a specific project structure rather than the wider CoreCo enterprise. The legal documents tell us what creditors received; they do not automatically tell us what all of those claims were worth.

That is where the restructuring stops being a balance-sheet story and starts becoming a valuation story.

Brazil is the number I would watch

NFE spent years building and acquiring a Brazilian LNG and power platform. At restructuring, that business was separated and its equity transferred to the creditors.

The transfer itself is clear. The harder question is sitting underneath it: what was BrazilCo actually worth when that equity changed hands?

That number would put the $5.7 billion debt reduction into a very different perspective.
