What Was Backing Prax's Money?

Prax's expansion was accompanied by a series of increasingly large and more complex securitisation arrangements.

The public record shows several stages in that financing programme: a US$750 million securitisation facility announced in 2021, a facility increased from £575 million to £739 million in 2022, and an arrangement described by Prax as a $1 billion securitisation facility in 2023. These figures should not be added together. The available record does not yet establish exactly how the facilities relate to one another.

Then State Oil, the parent company behind Prax, entered administration in 2025.

Administrators later reported material irregularities connected with a major receivables facility. Public reporting described a facility of roughly £783 million and a large gap between the amounts drawn and the receivables or invoices that were supposed to support them.

The obvious question is what happened to the money.

The more useful question comes earlier:

What was actually backing it?

A financing structure built alongside the expansion

Prax's financing story accelerated alongside the group's expansion.

In 2021, Prax acquired the Lindsey Oil Refinery and associated logistics assets from Total. Trafigura subsequently announced an exclusive arrangement to supply crude oil and refinery feedstock to Lindsey.

That same year, Prax announced a US$750 million securitisation facility.

The following year, Prax announced that its securitisation facility had been increased from £575 million to £739 million. HSBC, Citi, JPMorgan Chase and Royal Bank of Canada were identified as senior note purchasers.

In June 2023, Prax announced an extension and amendment of what it described as a $1 billion securitisation facility. The arrangement was extended to February 2025 and additional Prax entities in Switzerland, Singapore and the United States were brought into the structure.

A year later, Prax announced another facility: US$750 million of securitisation financing.

How the structure worked

The structure was built around Prax Global Financial Services DAC, an Irish special-purpose vehicle.

Under the disclosed structure, several Prax entities sold trade receivables — money owed to those businesses by customers — into the arrangement. The SPV then issued securities to financial institutions.

A receivables securitisation uses an underlying pool of receivables as part of the financing structure. The quality, eligibility and collection of those receivables therefore matter to the financing itself.

That is why the events of 2025 are important.

The paper trail shortly before the collapse

There is a useful glimpse of the structure in records of the International Stock Exchange (TISE).

An October 2024 notice reported aggregate senior notes of approximately £422.3 million and mezzanine notes of approximately £39.6 million associated with Prax Global Financial Services DAC. The corrected senior-note figure was £422,289,894, while the mezzanine notes were £39,647,589.

Senior and mezzanine notes are different layers of the financing structure: senior notes sit higher in the repayment hierarchy, while mezzanine notes take more risk.

Those figures should not be treated as a simple replacement for the earlier £739 million or $1 billion figures.

The public record currently does not establish that these amounts represent a single borrowing stack moving neatly from one size to another. Some may reflect different stages, currencies, note classes or related facilities.

The first task in understanding Prax's financing is therefore not to add the headline numbers together. It is to reconstruct the structure.

The gap reported after the collapse

That reconstruction looks very different after the 2025 administration.

Administrators reported material irregularities involving a major receivables facility. Global Trade Review reported a large gap between the facility's drawings and the receivables or invoices supporting those drawings. The final lender loss was not publicly quantified in the material available to the investigation.

The Guardian separately reported on a £783 million facility secured against expected oil-product sales and described irregularities identified by administrators.

That changes the significance of the earlier disclosures.

The financing announcements were public. The SPV was identifiable. The note structure was visible. The expansion of the financing programme was visible.

What those announcements did not reveal, at least from the material currently available, was the full quality and composition of the receivables supporting the financing.

What the public record can show

The existing documents allow part of the structure to be reconstructed: there was an operating group, receivables generated by multiple entities, a special-purpose vehicle into which those receivables fed, and securities issued by the SPV and purchased by international financial institutions.

The financing structure was repeatedly amended and expanded as Prax grew. After the group's collapse, administrators reported material irregularities in a major facility connected with that financing structure.

That makes the financing structure an important part of the story of Prax's collapse.

The administrators' account also points to refinery downtime, lower refining margins, heavy capital outlays and tax pressure as contributors to the group's deteriorating financial position.

But it is not enough to answer every question.

What remains unresolved

The available public material does not yet establish the exact receivables pool behind the facility, the advance rate, the complete eligibility tests applied to invoices, or exactly how collection accounts were controlled. It also does not provide a definitive public figure for the alleged shortfall.

The public record does not yet establish precisely how the £783 million facility referred to in administrator reporting relates to every earlier and later securitisation disclosure.

Those are not minor technicalities.

They are the difference between knowing that a financing structure existed and knowing exactly how it worked.

The audit trail

The financing questions are also why the Financial Reporting Council's September 2026 announcement matters.

The FRC opened an investigation into an individual accountant's conduct concerning State Oil's financing arrangements from February 2021 through June 2025. It also opened investigations into KPMG's audits of State Oil for the years ended February 2022 and February 2023, and PKF Littlejohn's audit for the year ended February 2024.

The announcement does not disclose the regulator's conclusions.

The FRC investigation therefore should not be treated as evidence that the auditors failed, or that misconduct has been established.

What it does establish is narrower:

the financing arrangements and the financial reporting around them are now the subject of formal regulatory scrutiny.

What Prax's public record leaves us with

The most useful way to understand the Prax collapse may not be to start with the collapse itself.

It is to look at the financing structure that preceded it.

Between 2021 and 2024, Prax publicly described a securitisation programme that became larger, more international and more complex. Behind that programme sat an SPV and receivables generated by operating entities. By 2025, administrators were reporting material irregularities in a major facility connected with those receivables.

The public record can show much of the architecture.

It cannot yet fully show what happened inside it.
