The AI Trade Had a Second Side: Inside the Rise and Unwind of Situational Awareness

How a young AI-focused fund built a multibillion-dollar portfolio around the infrastructure boom — and what became visible when the trade had to unwind.

In July 2026, Citadel began buying a portfolio that had taken Situational Awareness less than two years to build.

The seller was not an old hedge fund caught on the wrong side of a trade. Situational Awareness had been founded in 2024 by Leopold Aschenbrenner, a former OpenAI employee who built the fund around a straightforward thesis: the consequences of artificial intelligence would extend far beyond software. The companies supplying chips, electricity, cooling, networking and computing capacity could become some of the biggest beneficiaries of the AI boom.

That bet attracted capital quickly.

By the middle of 2026, Situational Awareness was reporting more than $20 billion of reportable securities in its latest 13F filing.

Then the AI trade turned.

The portfolio that had been assembled around the expansion of AI infrastructure became one of the largest forced-position stories of the summer. Reports described financing pressure around the fund, JPMorgan curtailing lending, and Citadel taking on most of the stock portfolio.

The interesting part is not simply that a large fund lost money.

It is how the trade got that large in the first place — and what the unwind reveals about the financial machinery underneath the AI boom.

1. The Fund That Came Out of Nowhere

Situational Awareness appeared in public filings in 2024.

Its first onshore fund filing was made in September of that year, with the first sale reported on November 1. By September 2025, a Form D amendment showed roughly $998 million sold.

Six months later, a March 2026 amendment put the figure at approximately $1.76 billion, across 93 investors.

The adviser kept growing.

Its June 2026 ADV reported roughly $9.28 billion in regulatory assets under management.

The public securities portfolio was expanding even faster.

The 13F filing for the end of 2024 showed roughly $255 million in reportable securities.

Then:

$1.01 billion in Q1 2025.
$2.12 billion in Q2.
$4.14 billion in Q3.
$5.52 billion in Q4.

By Q1 2026, the filing had reached approximately $13.68 billion.

The Q2 filing, submitted in August, showed approximately $20.24 billion.

A fund founded in 2024 had, in less than two years, become a multibillion-dollar public-market operation.

And the portfolio gave away something about how Aschenbrenner viewed the AI cycle.

2. It Wasn't Really a Bet on AI Companies

The first holdings did not look like a conventional software portfolio.

There was Marvell, Vertiv, Vistra, Talen Energy, Constellation Energy, Modine.

The companies sat at very different points in the market, but they shared something: each had a relationship to the physical buildout required for more computing.

Power generation, Data-centre equipment, Cooling, Networking, Semiconductors, Infrastructure.

As the portfolio expanded, the theme became broader. CoreWeave appeared.

So did Core Scientific, Applied Digital, IREN, Bloom Energy, Coherent, Lumentum, Riot Platforms, Sandisk and TSMC.

The portfolio also moved into semiconductor names and options, including positions tied to Nvidia, Micron, Broadcom, AMD, Oracle, ASML and other parts of the semiconductor chain.

This made the original thesis clearer.

The bet was not simply that AI companies would make more money.

It was that the world would have to spend extraordinary amounts of money to build the machinery needed for AI. That meant the opportunity could sit one step below the headline companies.

A data-centre operator could benefit. So could a power producer. A networking company. A chip manufacturer. A cooling supplier. A company controlling access to computing capacity.

The portfolio was beginning to look less like an AI stock basket and more like a map of the infrastructure boom.

Then the options complicated the picture again.

3. The Fund Wasn't Just Buying the Winners

By 2025, Situational Awareness was also using derivatives to express views around the semiconductor complex.

The quarterly filings included large put positions.

Secondary analysis of the Q1 2026 filing estimated approximately $8.46 billion of semiconductor put exposure across names including SMH, Nvidia, Broadcom, AMD, Oracle, ASML, TSMC and Micron.

Those positions could have served different purposes inside a much larger portfolio: downside bets, hedges, or part of more complex combinations.

But one thing was clear from the filings.

The fund was not simply buying companies that stood to benefit from AI spending and holding them.

It was trading around the consequences of the same cycle. That distinction became important as the portfolio grew. A long position can be explained by a company's expected earnings. A portfolio combining infrastructure stocks with large option positions raises a different question:

How was the whole thing being financed?

4. The Part You Couldn't See in the 13F

A 13F tells you what securities were reportable at a particular moment.

It does not show the entire financial life of a fund. That distinction becomes much more interesting once the portfolio is measured in tens of billions.

Reports around Situational Awareness's 2026 stress described relationships with Goldman Sachs, JPMorgan, Citigroup and Bank of America, followed later by a prime-brokerage relationship with Clear Street.

The financing itself remained behind the portfolio.

Which securities were pledged?

How much borrowing sat against them?

How much room existed before a lender demanded more collateral?

And when the market started moving quickly, which part of the structure came under pressure first?

Those questions are usually invisible when looking at a quarterly holdings report.

The July episode brought them much closer to the surface.

Reuters reported that JPMorgan curtailed lending to the fund after losses tied to its AI positions.

Bloomberg reported that Situational Awareness established a relationship with Clear Street after the near-collapse of its leveraged AI positions.

Other reporting placed the fund's peak exposure at roughly $45 billion and described leverage of around 4×.

Those figures are secondary reports rather than a public financing ledger.

But they change the way the 13F should be read.

A $20 billion securities portfolio and $20 billion of investor capital are not the same thing. And a portfolio financed partly through borrowing behaves differently when the market moves sharply in the wrong direction.

5. The Companies Underneath the Trade Were Leveraged Too

There was another layer.

The same AI boom that drove investors into infrastructure companies was encouraging those companies to borrow heavily to build the infrastructure itself.

CoreWeave is the clearest example.

By June 2026, CoreWeave reported approximately $35.6 billion of debt and $103.7 billion of remaining performance obligations.

Earlier SEC filings showed an $8.5 billion delayed-draw term loan facility, secured by infrastructure including GPU servers.

The business model was increasingly turning expected future demand for computing into financing capacity today.

Customers commit to computing. Those commitments support revenue expectations. Infrastructure gets built. The infrastructure provides collateral. The collateral supports borrowing. And the borrowing funds still more infrastructure.

That is one reason CoreWeave is useful for understanding the financial side of the AI buildout. The company's growth story was not just an equity story. It was also a debt story.

Nebius provides another example. Its filings disclosed a $775 million senior secured term loan backed by GPU infrastructure and contracted cash flows. The company later raised approximately $4.34 billion in convertible debt.

Applied Digital disclosed $1.59 billion of senior secured notes to fund its Polaris Forge 1 data-centre project, alongside additional secured financing.

Different companies. Different structures. Same broader cycle.

The investor sees an AI infrastructure stock. The company sees an opportunity to borrow against infrastructure and future cash flows.

The lender sees collateral. The customer sees computing capacity.

And underneath all of them is the assumption that demand for AI infrastructure will continue to expand.

6. Then the Market Turned

That assumption became much harder to take for granted in July. The AI-related selloff hit companies across the ecosystem at the same time.

For an ordinary investor, falling prices mean a smaller portfolio. For a leveraged investor, falling prices can also mean shrinking collateral.

That difference is crucial.

A position can remain fundamentally attractive and still become a financing problem if its market value falls quickly enough.

Situational Awareness had built positions around exactly the part of the market that had expanded most aggressively.

Now the direction had changed.

JPMorgan's lending relationship was reportedly curtailed.

The fund's public portfolio was under pressure. And suddenly the question was no longer what those individual stocks were worth over the next five years.

It was who could take the entire position now.

7. Citadel Takes the Other Side

The answer, at least for much of the public-equity portfolio, was Citadel.

Reuters reported on July 30 that Citadel bought most of Situational Awareness's stock holdings after the AI rout. The reported value of the portfolio was around $16 billion.

That number is easy to misunderstand. It was the reported value of the public-equity book involved in the transaction — not an established purchase price.

The difference matters.

A portfolio can have a market value of $16 billion and still change hands for less if the seller needs immediate liquidity. And that appears to be what made the transaction unusual.

The reported discount to prevailing market prices was not merely a side detail. It was the price of moving a huge amount of risk quickly.

Later, Ken Griffin said Citadel had reduced more than 80% of the aggregate risk it had taken on and had executed roughly 100 block trades, representing more than $4 billion in value.

That gives the transaction a different character. Citadel was not simply “buying stocks.”

It was taking a large, concentrated bundle of market risk and then breaking it apart again.

The seller needed certainty. The buyer needed room to manage the positions. The gap between those two needs is where the discount lives. And that is where the cost of a forced unwind becomes visible.

8. What Had Been Built Was Bigger Than a Stock Portfolio

Look backward from that transaction and the pieces begin to line up.

A fund raises capital. It builds positions in companies positioned around AI infrastructure. It adds derivatives around parts of the semiconductor chain. Those positions sit inside a financing system involving prime brokers and lenders.

The companies underneath the positions are themselves raising billions against GPUs, data centres, contracted revenues and other infrastructure.

Then the market turns. Stock prices fall. Collateral values change. Financing becomes more important.

And a portfolio that had been accumulated over months needs to move in a matter of days.

From one angle, it is a hedge-fund liquidation. From another, it is a stress test of the entire financing chain around the AI boom.

That is what makes the Situational Awareness case unusually revealing. The fund did not create the AI capital cycle. It sat inside it.

9. The Unwind Changes the Way the AI Boom Looks

For most of the boom, the public story was straightforward.

AI demand was rising. Companies needed more chips. More chips required more data centres. Data centres required electricity, cooling, networking and land. Investors found the companies selling those things.

The financial side was less visible.

Capital was also being used to finance the infrastructure. Debt was being raised against assets. Future contracts were being turned into borrowing capacity. Prime brokers were financing market positions.

And large investors were using derivatives to express views on where the cycle might overheat.

None of those pieces needed to move together every day. But they were connected.

Situational Awareness became interesting precisely because the connections became visible when they were put under pressure.

The $20.24 billion 13F was the visible portfolio.

The reported ~$45 billion peak exposure points to something larger around it.

The $9.28 billion regulatory AUM belongs to yet another measurement.

The ~$16 billion Citadel transaction value describes the public-equity book at a particular moment.

They are not interchangeable numbers.

Together, they describe an investment operation whose public footprint had become much larger and more complicated than a simple list of stocks would suggest.

10. The Question Left Behind

Situational Awareness began with a view about where artificial intelligence was going. The fund found a way to express that view across power, chips, data centres, compute and the companies building them.

Capital followed. The portfolio expanded. Financing became part of the picture.

Then the same market that had rewarded the trade turned against it.

By the time Citadel was buying the portfolio, the story was no longer simply about which AI companies were going to win. It was about who could absorb the risk when the market stopped making room for everyone at once.

That is the part of the AI boom that a holdings report cannot show on its own.

The visible trade was in the stocks. The bigger trade was in the capital supporting everything underneath them.
