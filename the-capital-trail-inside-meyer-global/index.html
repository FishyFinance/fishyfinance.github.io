The Capital Trail Inside Meyer Global

On January 3, 2024, a Meyer Global-managed fund faced a $46,020 capital call tied to a SpaceX investment.

The call was not met.

The Securities and Exchange Commission says the result was the forfeiture of an investment worth nearly $3 million.

That gap — $46,020 owed against an investment valued at nearly $3 million — is where the Meyer Global story becomes larger than a single missed payment.

The money had entered a network of private funds built to give investors access to companies such as SpaceX, OpenAI and other pre-IPO businesses. On September 30, 2026, the SEC alleged that some of the money moving through that network did not remain on the investment path.

This account follows those movements: from the funds investors were asked to join, into private-market investments, and then into the transactions described in the SEC's complaint.

Where a transaction is reported by a credible source rather than directly documented in the SEC filings, it is identified as such. Where the SEC is making an allegation, it is treated as an allegation.

The fund behind the SpaceX investment

The SEC says Owen E.H. Meyer began raising capital as early as November 2019 for private funds that would acquire interests in pre-IPO companies. The first vehicle was Starship I.

By the time described in the SEC complaint, Meyer Global Management — or MGM — had created approximately 16 affiliated private funds. The funds were generally designed around individual pre-IPO investments. Rather than always holding company shares directly, they could invest through another fund or adviser that held the underlying securities.

That structure helps explain why a SpaceX investment could sit several layers away from the individual who supplied the money.

SEC Form D filings show the machinery in pieces.

Starship X, for example, identifies Meyer Equity LLC as an executive officer and director and Owen Meyer as an executive officer, director, promoter and managing partner. Another filing for Starship 11 identifies Meyer Equity LLC as general partner and Owen Meyer as a related person.

The scale was larger than any one filing.

Bloomberg Law reported that the SEC alleges MGM raised at least $18.5 million from nearly 100 investors. An AUM13F record separately associates the manager or sponsor with 23 private funds. The SEC's complaint refers to approximately 16 affiliated funds. Those figures come from different records and descriptions of the fund universe; they do not, by themselves, establish that money is missing.

Some individual filings show capital entering the system.

Starship III reported $1,028,225 in securities sold. Starship IV reported $323,728. Starship 11 reported $564,702, while Destinus I reported $100,000.

Together, those four reported amounts total $2,016,655 — a calculation from selected filings, not a measure of MGM's total fundraising.

The investments were not confined to SpaceX

One of the funds was also connected to PlayStar Gaming Group AB.

In December 2022, PlayStar announced a $15 million investment, with reporting identifying Meyer Global as a minority investor. PlayStar said the funding would support its New Jersey operations and planned Pennsylvania expansion. 

The investment later encountered a different transaction.

Glitnor acquired a 37.5% stake in PlayStar.

If a Meyer-related fund held part of PlayStar when that transaction happened, what happened to that fund's position?

The investment and the later Glitnor transaction are documented. The complete economics of the Meyer vehicle's entry position, subsequent ownership and disposition are not.

That question becomes more interesting when the SEC's allegations are followed back into the accounts connected to the Meyer funds.

Then the money starts moving differently

The SEC alleges that, beginning at least in December 2021, Meyer and MGM misused assets from MGM-managed funds and misled investors. In one scheme, the SEC alleges that investor proceeds were diverted and that investors were required to sign releases accepting less than they were owed before receiving any money. In another, the SEC alleges that investor statements were inflated. 

Some of the transactions are unusually specific.

On April 12, the SEC alleges that $35,000 moved from an account associated with the PlayStar investment to Meyer's personal account.

Twelve days later, another $30,000 moved from the same PlayStar-related account to his personal account.

Then came a smaller transaction that makes the sequence unusually easy to follow.

A $4,415.56 charge at a strip club was attempted on a Meyer Global Partners card. The charge was declined. A $10,000 transfer was then made into the account, and the payment went through minutes later.

The SEC alleges these transactions as part of the broader misuse of fund assets. They are not adjudicated findings.

The complaint describes other transfers with similarly specific trails.

The SEC says Meyer transferred $636,000 from the Liquidated SpaceX Funds to a personal account. From that money, the complaint says, he spent thousands of dollars at Bloomingdale's and Amazon between November 2025 and February 2026.

Another $23,000 wire went to an individual with a memo referencing “Destinus Funding Investments.” Destinus was held by another MGM-managed fund, while the Liquidated SpaceX Funds did not invest in Destinus.

Between November 2025 and January 2026, the SEC says six additional wires totaling $40,000 went to another individual. The recipient, according to the complaint, was not an investor in the Liquidated SpaceX Funds.

In December 2025, another $86,000 went to Meyer's father. The memo included “Oikoi Network Fund – Return of Funds.” The SEC says Meyer's father was not an investor in the Liquidated SpaceX Funds. 

Taken together, these are not simply fund names appearing in a database. They are individual transfers: amounts, dates, recipients, account movements and, in several cases, transaction descriptions.

The SpaceX loss looks different when viewed alongside those transfers

Return to the $46,020 capital call.

The SEC alleges that an MGM-managed fund faced a capital-call deficiency connected to its SpaceX investment. The fund did not meet the call, and the SEC says the result was forfeiture of an investment worth nearly $3 million. 

A capital call is not itself unusual. Private-market investments can require a fund to supply additional capital after an initial commitment.

What matters here is what happened when that obligation was not met.

The SEC says the fund lost the SpaceX investment.

An obligation of $46,020 therefore stood between the fund and an asset that the SEC valued at nearly $3 million.

The underlying investment chain was more complicated than those two numbers suggest. The SEC says the funds could invest through other advisers or vehicles rather than directly holding every underlying security. 

The complete contractual sequence — the exact investment vehicle, original acquisition terms, capital-call notice, deadline, forfeiture provision and subsequent recipient of the interest — is not contained in the material reviewed for this investigation.

That last step matters because forfeiture tells us what happened to the fund's position. It does not, by itself, tell us who ended up with the economic interest.

The names become important again

The capital did not move through one entity called Meyer Global.

Meyer Equity LLC appears in fund filings. Oikoi Network Partners appears across multiple Starship vehicles. Meyer Global Management appears as the investment manager in regulatory records. Meyer Global GP LLC exists as a separate entity associated with the broader structure. 

The same problem appears in the PlayStar records.

One source refers to an MGP I fund, while other material refers to Meyer Global Partners. The available evidence does not establish that those names should automatically be treated as the same legal vehicle.

That distinction matters because the entity holding an investment is the entity whose economics have to be followed.

A brand name can tell you where to look.

The legal entity tells you where the asset sits.

Three trails, one unresolved question

The PlayStar investment is one trail: money raised, investment made, and a later transaction in which Glitnor acquired a 37.5% stake.

The SEC's alleged PlayStar-account transfers are another: $35,000, then $30,000, moving from the account associated with the investment into Meyer's personal account, followed by the strip-club payment sequence.

The SpaceX episode is a third: investment acquired, a $46,020 capital call issued, the obligation allegedly left unmet, and an interest worth nearly $3 million forfeited.

The SEC's other alleged transfers — including the $636,000 movement into Meyer's personal account, the $23,000 Destinus wire, the six $40,000 wires and the $86,000 payment to his father — add further points to the capital trail.

The transactions do not all have the same explanation. But they can be followed through the entities, accounts and investment vehicles named in the underlying records.

And at the end of the SpaceX trail, one question remains:

Who ultimately received the forfeited SpaceX interest, and on what terms?
