Mars FX’s $566.7 Million Withdrawal Request Raises a Basic Question: Who Actually Held the Assets?

On November 11, 2024, Novus, acting on behalf of Mars FX Master Fund, asked for roughly $566.73 million to be withdrawn from a trading arrangement it believed represented the value of its investment.

The request covered 2,250 trading accounts held in the name of Mars FX Master Fund, according to restructuring materials now before courts in the United States and the British Virgin Islands. The deadline was January 30, 2025. The money was not paid.

Within weeks, Mars FX told investors that it could no longer operate in the ordinary course. The Cayman entities suspended redemptions. The group eventually entered insolvency proceedings.

Today, the $566.73 million figure sits at the centre of a much harder question.

What, exactly, did that number represent — and where were the underlying assets actually held?

The answer matters because the documents do not describe a simple structure in which Mars FX held $566.73 million in a bank or brokerage account in its own name. They describe a chain involving a U.S. feeder fund, a Cayman master fund, Hong Kong banks and trust companies, a trading-platform operator, and brokerage accounts held in the names of entities connected to that operator.

The distinction between those things — an investment value, a platform balance, a legal account, and independently verified assets — is where this story becomes difficult.

The money started with a conventional fund structure

Mars FX US LP was formed in Delaware in 2020 as the U.S. vehicle in a master-feeder structure. Its 2024 SEC Form D amendment reported that the partnership had sold $331.08 million of fund interests to 426 investors, up from $239.32 million in 2023, $146.64 million in 2022 and $44.37 million in 2021. The filing identifies Novus Capital Partners LLC as the fund's general partner and names David Choi, Ashish Patel, Patrick Hofmann and Jae Y. Choi among the related persons.

A Form D is not an SEC audit of a fund. The SEC's filing itself states that the Commission has not necessarily reviewed the information or determined that it is accurate and complete.

The structure itself was straightforward enough. The movement of capital was more complicated.

Mars FX US was the onshore feeder. Mars FX International was the offshore feeder. Mars FX Master Ltd, in the Cayman Islands, was the master fund through which the trading strategy was supposed to operate. Novus served as general partner of the U.S. vehicle and investment manager of the Cayman entities. A January 31, 2025 shareholder register recorded Mars FX US as owning 75.64% of the Master Fund, represented by 1,464,828.2367 Class A shares at a stated NAV price of $296.4860 per share, or approximately $434.3 million.

$277 million was sent into the trading arrangement

According to materials summarised in the Mars FX Chapter 11 record, the U.S. debtor and the two Cayman entities collectively invested approximately $277.371 million into a trading structure operated by Tech RealFX Ltd., or TRFX, between August 2020 and August 2024.

The available reconstruction identifies two major routes from the U.S. fund into Hong Kong.

Between August 2, 2020 and February 25, 2025, Mars FX transferred approximately $122.336 million from accounts at JPMorgan Chase and Northern Trust to Bank of Communications (Hong Kong).

Between April 7, 2022 and August 1, 2024, it transferred another $131.25 million from Northern Trust to accounts at Legacy Trust Company in Hong Kong.

Those two numbers total approximately $253.6 million. They should not, however, simply be treated as a second, independent measure of money invested. The materials describe them as historical transfers over overlapping periods, while the $277.371 million figure is described as the collective investment of the debtor and Cayman entities. Without the complete bank ledger and transaction-level reconciliation, the gross transfer totals cannot safely be treated as a clean measure of unique capital deployed.

That distinction is important because the documentary trail does not yet produce a single, perfectly reconciled line that can be labelled “investor money in” and another that can be labelled “money out.”

Instead, it produces a chain.

Investors → Mars FX entities → JPMorgan / Northern Trust → BOCOM / Legacy Trust → TRFX-related structure → brokerage accounts → reported trading balance.

The brokerage accounts were not in the Master Fund’s name

This is one of the most consequential details in the record.

The restructuring materials identify brokerage accounts at Equiti Capital UK and GO Markets. But the accounts were held in the names of TRFX, 4XHUB Limited and Treal Capital Limited, rather than directly in the name of Mars FX Master Ltd. Trading from those accounts was understood to have taken place through TRFX's technology platform.

That creates a distinction between the person or entity named on an account and the party asserting an economic entitlement to the assets in that account.

The Mars FX materials say that certain balances in those accounts may have been held for the benefit of the Master Fund and, indirectly, the U.S. feeder. But the same materials say that the beneficial ownership of the relevant bank, trust and brokerage accounts remains under investigation.

There is another layer of uncertainty. The available records identify similarly named companies across jurisdictions, including TRFX and Treal entities in the BVI and Hong Kong and 4XHUB entities in Labuan and Hong Kong. The restructuring record says investigations are continuing into which legal entities actually owned the relevant accounts.

In other words, the question is not simply whether money entered a trading platform.

It is also:

Whose account was it in?
Who had legal title?
Who had beneficial ownership?
And who could actually withdraw it?

Those are different questions.

Then came the $566.73 million withdrawal request

The number that eventually brought the structure into focus was substantially larger than the roughly $277.4 million described as cumulative investment into the TRFX trading arrangement.

On November 11, 2024, Novus, acting on behalf of the Master Fund, submitted a withdrawal request seeking approximately $566.73 million covering all 2,250 trading accounts described as being held in the name of the Master Fund. The amount represented the claimed platform balance, including purported trading profits. The requested payment deadline was January 30, 2025.

That makes the $566.73 million figure specific to the Mars FX Master Fund's asserted position. It is not presented in the restructuring record as the total value of TRFX's entire client platform.

But specificity does not make it cash.

A withdrawal request demonstrates that Mars FX asserted an entitlement to a particular amount. It does not, by itself, establish that $566.73 million existed as withdrawable cash in an independently verified account.

The gap between those two propositions is the central evidentiary issue.

The number was represented as an asset before it became a disputed claim

The earlier audited financial statements make the issue more difficult.

The Master Fund's 2022 financial statements reported $248.09 million of total cash at bank and broker. The corresponding 2023 figure was $381.35 million.

For Mars FX US, the investment in the Master Fund was recorded at fair value of $207.35 million in 2022 and $325.27 million in 2023, accompanied by cash at bank of $8.56 million and $7.32 million respectively. The audit reports for those years were unqualified, according to the restructuring materials.

Those statements matter because they show that substantial assets were being reported through the fund's accounting system before the withdrawal failure.

They do not, by themselves, answer where the economic assets were sitting in the external financial system.

The distinction became particularly important because the liquidators later began investigating the audit process itself.

The restructuring record says the Joint Official Liquidators are investigating potential professional-negligence and breach-of-contract claims against Deloitte relating to the 2022 and 2023 audits. It also says there is a sufficient basis, on the information then available, to investigate whether audit work adequately verified balances represented as being held with brokers or through associated material.

That is an investigation, not a finding that Deloitte failed in its duties.

And the later court order seeking Deloitte's records is likewise not a finding of wrongdoing. In the Mars FX Master Chapter 15 case, a U.S. Bankruptcy Court order entered on August 3, 2026 authorised the foreign representatives to issue subpoenas and examine Deloitte & Touche in connection with the restructuring.

What the audit records ultimately establish remains an open question.

The documents also disagree about what happened at TRFX

The legal dispute with TRFX adds another layer.

After the withdrawal request went unpaid, statutory demands were served in February 2025. TRFX subsequently challenged the underlying arrangements, arguing, among other things, that agreements had been executed by an individual who lacked authority, that certain correspondence and broker statements relied upon by Mars FX were forged, and that there was no evidence that the Master Fund had transferred the relevant funds to TRFX.

That application was later discontinued.

Mars FX and the Cayman entities then commenced BVI proceedings seeking recovery of approximately $566.73 million. TRFX's defence, filed in November 2025, disputed the authenticity or authority underlying key documents, denied receiving or holding client monies and advanced a materially different account of the trading history and balances. The proceedings remain ongoing.

One particularly important allegation concerns TRFX's platform itself. The restructuring materials note unresolved questions around a change in the website domain hosting the trading platform around October 2022 and competing accounts concerning operational control.

None of that establishes that the reported trading results were fictitious.

It does establish something narrower and more useful: the parties now disagree materially about the underlying agreements, authority, custody and trading history that support the claimed balance.

Those disagreements have to be resolved through documents and external records, not inferred from the size of the final number.

The balance sheet now tells a different story

By January 31, 2025, the Master Fund's shareholder register recorded Mars FX US's 75.64% interest at $434.3 million. The investor register for Mars FX US recorded approximately $432.63 million of aggregate investor NAV across 535 positions.

At first glance, those numbers can appear connected to the $566.73 million withdrawal request, but they measure different things at different points in time.

The $434.3 million figure is the recorded value of Mars FX US's stake in the Master Fund.

The $432.63 million figure is the recorded aggregate NAV of the U.S. vehicle's investors.

The $566.73 million figure is the claimed combined platform position that Novus sought to withdraw on behalf of the Master Fund in November 2024.

And the approximately $277.37 million figure describes cumulative investment by the debtor and Cayman entities into the TRFX trading arrangement through August 2024.

These numbers should not be forced into a single reconciliation before the underlying records support one.

What they do show is the scale of the problem. A capital deployment of roughly $277 million was associated with a reported platform position of roughly $567 million. That difference is not automatically unexplained money, and it is not automatically proven trading profit. It represents a claimed economic increase whose underlying transaction and valuation history now matters.

The question is whether the documentary record can bridge those numbers.

What is independently established — and what is not

The Mars FX case now sits inside several legal systems at once.

The U.S. Chapter 11 case for Mars FX US was filed on March 23, 2026. On September 21, 2026, the U.S. Bankruptcy Court for the Southern District of New York entered an order confirming Mars FX US's First Amended Plan of Liquidation.

The Cayman Master Fund is proceeding under Chapter 15 recognition proceedings in the same court, alongside the Cayman liquidation process. The BVI litigation against TRFX remains a principal recovery route.

The practical task for the estate is therefore not merely to determine whether a number appeared in a financial statement.

It is to reconstruct the chain from the original capital to whatever assets, claims or rights remain recoverable today.

That reconstruction requires several separate answers. There must be bank records showing what left Mars FX accounts. There must be records showing what arrived at BOCOM and Legacy Trust. There must be documents establishing which legal entities controlled the receiving accounts. There must be broker statements and broker-side records establishing what Equiti and GO Markets actually held. There must be evidence establishing who owned those brokerage balances. And there must be sufficient audit documentation to establish what evidence was available to the auditors when the 2022 and 2023 balances were reported. Only after those steps can the economic story be completed.

The unanswered question is narrower than “where did the money go?”

That broader question assumes the money disappeared in a particular way.

The records available today do not establish that.

The more precise question is harder — and potentially more revealing:

At each stage between Mars FX's investors and the claimed $566.73 million platform balance, what asset or contractual right existed, who legally controlled it, and what independent evidence verified it?

The distinction matters. Investor capital can become a fund interest. A fund interest can become an investment recorded at fair value. A fund can assert a claim against a trading counterparty. A trading platform can report an account balance. A broker can maintain an account in another entity's name. And an auditor can receive evidence purporting to support a balance.

Those statements can all be true at the same time without establishing that the final economic asset was readily recoverable by the investor.

That is the problem Mars FX is now trying to solve through liquidation, litigation and discovery.

The $566.73 million figure is therefore best understood not as the end of the investigation, but as the beginning of it.

The courts will ultimately determine the legal claims. The brokers and other record holders may provide the transaction history. The audit records may clarify what was independently checked and how.

For now, the restructuring record reviewed here reports a large claimed asset position, substantial historical transfers and a complicated custody structure.

It does not yet provide a complete, independently verified bridge from one to the other.

That bridge is the missing piece.
