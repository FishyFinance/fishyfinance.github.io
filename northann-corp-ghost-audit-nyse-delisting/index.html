Northann Filed an Audit Opinion Its Auditor Says It Never Authorized

Northann Corp.'s annual report contained something investors normally take for granted: an auditor's opinion.

The auditor later said it had never authorized it.

Northann filed its FY2025 Form 10-K with the SEC on April 14, 2026. The filing presented financial statements accompanied by an audit report attributed to LAO Professionals.

Later, the accounting firm said the document did not represent its work.

In a letter filed with the SEC, LAO said it had resigned on June 8 because its name and audit opinion had been used without authorization. The firm said it had not prepared, issued, signed, authorized or consented to the audit opinion included in Northann's filing.

That creates a question more basic than whether one particular number in Northann's accounts was wrong:

How did an audit opinion attributed to an accounting firm enter a public filing when the firm says it never authorized it?

The answer is not established by the current record. But the consequences are.

The audit had not been completed

LAO's later filing adds an important detail.

The firm said it had not obtained sufficient appropriate audit evidence to complete the audit or issue an opinion. According to LAO, it repeatedly requested documents needed to perform its procedures. LAO said Northann provided inaccessible links and declined requests to send certain large document sets as email attachments.

This was not a case of an auditor completing its work and later discovering an error in the accounts. According to LAO, the audit itself had not been completed.

Northann subsequently disclosed that LAO had not identified specific financial-statement items as incorrect. But because of LAO's statements about the audit report, Northann told investors not to rely on its FY2024 and FY2025 financial statements pending a new audit.

The distinction matters: the current evidence establishes a dispute over the audit process and authorization of the report. It does not, by itself, establish that every number in Northann's financial statements is false.

The numbers are now provisional

The FY2025 10-K reported $13.6 million of revenue, $3.58 million of gross profit and an $11.67 million net loss.

It also reported approximately $1.03 million of cash at year-end and $5.68 million of cash used in operating activities during the year.

Those figures show what investors were presented with in the disputed filing.

They should not, however, be treated as final financial reality.

Northann itself has said the FY2024 and FY2025 financial statements should not be relied upon pending re-audit.

So the numbers occupy an unusual position: they remain part of the documentary record, but the company has withdrawn reliance on them while the accounting work is redone.

Then there is the $11 million software question

The reporting problem becomes more consequential when viewed alongside Northann's capital structure.

In November 2025, Northann agreed to acquire proprietary supply-chain software and intellectual property from Kingsford Consultancy for $5 million, payable through 12.5 million Northann shares.

It separately agreed to pay $6 million in shares for software and intellectual property under a development agreement with Asia Resource Holdings.

Together, those transactions represented:

$11 million of stated consideration for 27.5 million shares.

At December 31, 2025, Northann reported 22.93 million common shares outstanding.

The subsequent issuance of 27.5 million shares was therefore approximately 120% of the company's year-end share count.

That calculation does not establish that either transaction was improper.

It shows why the accounting treatment and valuation of the transactions matter.

The software transactions were large relative to Northann's existing equity base. If the financial statements describing them need to be re-audited, the consequences extend beyond an accounting footnote. They can affect how investors understand the company's assets, equity and dilution.

The reporting problem became a market problem

The consequences did not remain inside Northann's financial statements.

Trading in Northann's securities was halted in June.

The company formed an Oversight Committee and retained outside counsel after the audit dispute emerged. Lin Li, who had held senior executive and board positions, subsequently left the company.

In August, Northann disclosed that NYSE American had commenced delisting proceedings, determining that the company was not suitable for continued listing under several provisions of the NYSE American Company Guide.

The company has requested an oral hearing to review that determination.

None of those events, by themselves, establishes fraud.

They show something narrower: a dispute over the integrity and completion of the company's financial reporting process developed into a broader governance, trading and listing problem.

A new auditor now has to rebuild the picture

On August 26, Northann's Audit Committee appointed TQ International, PLLC as its independent registered public accounting firm.

TQ is expected to audit the FY2025 financial statements and review interim financial information for March 31 and June 30, 2026.

That work now has two jobs.

The first is straightforward: determine what the company's financial statements should actually say.

The second is less straightforward and remains unanswered:

What happened to the audit opinion attributed to LAO that appeared in the original filing?

The documentary record currently establishes the contradiction.

Northann filed an annual report containing an audit opinion attributed to LAO.

LAO later said it had not prepared or authorized that opinion and had not completed the audit.

Northann subsequently told investors not to rely on the financial statements while a replacement auditor undertakes the work.

What the current record does not establish is who caused the disputed audit material to appear in the filing, whether anyone acted intentionally, or whether the underlying financial statements contain material accounting errors.

Those are separate questions, and they require separate evidence.

For now, the most important document may be the next audit itself.

Not because it will necessarily explain how the disputed opinion entered the filing, but because it should begin answering the more immediate question for investors:

What, if anything, in Northann's reported financial position can still be relied upon?
