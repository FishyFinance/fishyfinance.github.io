The 8-Day Magic Trick: How ₹25 Crore Created a ₹5,000 Crore Corporate Illusion

By FishyFinance Research & Analysis
Publication Date: August 20, 2026

Executive Summary & Regulatory Context: On August 19, 2026, the Securities and Exchange Board of India (SEBI) issued a 112-page interim order examining a complex network of fund routing, purported unsecured loans and preferential equity allotments involving Dhenu Buildcon Infra Limited. This investigation reconstructs the mechanism described in SEBI's findings and examines how a company with negligible operating activity became associated with a multi-thousand-crore valuation.

1. The ₹3 Crore Ghost Company

To understand the financial machinery that SEBI later examined, it helps to start with where Dhenu Buildcon Infra Limited actually stood before the transformation.

For years, Dhenu Buildcon Infra Limited (DBIL) occupied the quiet end of India's listed-company universe. Its operating profile was small, its revenues were negligible and its market value was measured in a few crore rupees rather than hundreds or thousands of crores.

Between FY2022 and FY2024, the company's financial profile remained broadly consistent with that picture: negligible operating revenue, limited business activity and a market capitalisation of roughly ₹3 crore.

That was the starting point. Then the numbers began to change.

On December 21, 2024, the company expanded its borrowing capacity to ₹1,000 crore. For a company operating at a fraction of that scale, the change was striking. The question was not simply why a company with such a small operating footprint would need the ability to borrow ₹1,000 crore. The more important question was where the money behind that borrowing would come from.

The answer, according to SEBI's subsequent examination, begins with a much smaller number. ₹25.05 crore.

2. The Seed: A ₹25 Crore Starting Pool

A ₹1,000-crore loan book normally implies access to substantial capital. But SEBI's examination of DBIL's bank transactions identified an initial pool of approximately ₹25.05 crore that, according to its findings, was repeatedly routed through a network of connected entities.

SEBI's order examined how funds moved among these entities and DBIL during the relevant period.

The important point is not that ₹25.05 crore is a small amount in absolute terms. It is that the figure was dramatically smaller than the ₹1,000 crore of purported loans that ultimately appeared in DBIL's books.

That difference is the heart of the investigation.

The question becomes simple:

How can roughly ₹25 crore produce the appearance of ₹1,000 crore of lending without a corresponding injection of fresh capital?

3. The 8-Day Loop: 46 Transactions

Between December 24 and December 31, 2024, DBIL received funds through 46 separate credit transactions from the seven entities identified as lenders.

SEBI's examination found that the transactions were not independent events in the ordinary sense. Instead, the regulator traced multiple routes through which funds moved between connected entities before returning to DBIL.

The seven entities shown as lenders to DBIL were Golkonda Aluminium Extrusions Limited, Edoptica Retail India Limited, Tiaan Consumer Limited, Twinkle Mercantiles & Credits Private Limited, Shanta Agencies Private Limited, Shri Niwas Leasing and Finance Limited, and Utsav Securities Limited. SEBI's findings describe a network in which funds were moved through a series of interconnected routes involving DBIL and other entities. They identified 28 distinct routing variations across the 46 transactions examined, creating the appearance of successive loan inflows.

In other words, the important variable was not simply how much money existed, but how many times that money could be moved and recorded as a transaction.

SEBI identified approximately ₹25.05 crore as the initial pool in its analysis. That pool was allegedly layered through numerous entities and routes before returning to DBIL. Across eight days, the process produced an aggregate of approximately ₹1,000 crore in purported unsecured loans.

The regulator's findings also noted that DBIL's maximum bank balance during the relevant period was only about ₹25.81 crore, despite the purported receipt of ₹1,000 crore.

That contrast is crucial.

The ledger could record a much larger cumulative flow because the same underlying capital was being moved repeatedly. The balance of actual money available at any single point did not grow in proportion to the cumulative value of the transactions.

4. 🐟 Fishy Insight: The Pass-the-Note Economy

The easiest way to understand circular fund routing is to forget corporate finance for a moment.

Imagine Kaushik, Akhil, Nitesh, Aanchal and Sahana sitting around a table.

Kaushik places a single ₹1,000 note on the table.

He lends it to Akhil.

Akhil passes the same ₹1,000 to Nitesh and records it as a loan.

Nitesh passes it to Aanchal.

Aanchal passes it to Sahana.

Sahana eventually returns the same ₹1,000 to Kaushik.

The group now has a long sequence of loan transactions recorded on paper, but there is still only ₹1,000 of actual cash sitting inside the system.

If the process is repeated ten times, the transaction records can become much larger than the original amount of money.

That is the basic intuition behind the circular-funding mechanism examined by SEBI in the Dhenu case.

The Dhenu investigation is considerably more complicated than five people passing one note around a table. It involved multiple corporate entities, bank accounts and different transaction routes. But the underlying question remains the same:

How much new money actually entered the system, and how much was simply moving around inside it?

5. The Equity Swap: Turning Purported Debt Into Shares

The next stage was more consequential.

Once DBIL had recorded approximately ₹1,000 crore of purported unsecured loans, the company had a substantial liability on its balance sheet. In 2025, the company proposed a preferential issue to restructure a large portion of those loans.

The proposed transaction involved approximately ₹840 crore of unsecured loans being converted into equity.

The company's filings described the issue as a conversion of unsecured loans into equity shares and proposed the issuance of 5,91,54,92,940 equity shares at ₹1.42 per share. The six entities were allocated shares in the following broad proportions:

  • Golkonda Aluminium Extrusions Limited
  • Shanta Agencies Private Limited
  • Tiaan Consumer Limited
  • Twinkle Mercantiles & Credits Private Limited
  • Shri Niwas Leasing and Finance Limited
  • Utsav Securities Limited

Together, the preferential issue represented approximately 99.70% of the company's diluted equity.

The transformation was therefore significant.

What began as purported unsecured lending was converted into equity ownership of a listed company.

And this is where the story moves from the balance sheet to the ownership structure.

6. From Debt to Ownership

The preferential issue did not simply remove a liability from DBIL's books.

It also dramatically changed who controlled the company's equity.

The six allottees collectively received 5.91 billion new shares, with the largest allocations going to Twinkle Mercantiles & Credits, Golkonda Aluminium, Shanta Agencies and Tiaan Consumer, followed by Shri Niwas Leasing and Finance and Utsav Securities. The company's own filings described the transaction as a restructuring of unsecured loans and stated that the conversion was intended to strengthen its capital structure.

From a purely structural perspective, the transaction can be reduced to three numbers:

₹840 crore
purported unsecured loans being converted

↓

5.91 billion shares
issued through preferential allotment

↓

99.70%
of diluted equity concentrated among six entities

That concentration became one of the most important features of the case.

SEBI's interim order later examined the six entities as preferential allottees and lenders in the broader network surrounding DBIL.

7. The Valuation Explosion

The capital structure was changing at the same time that DBIL's market valuation was undergoing an extraordinary transformation.

A company that had once carried a market capitalisation of roughly ₹3 crore became associated with a market capitalisation of approximately ₹4,925 crore, with the analysis in the case also referring to a value of roughly ₹5,667 crore based on the relevant VWAP calculation.

The scale of the change is difficult to comprehend without putting the two numbers side by side.

~₹3 crore became ₹4,925 crore

That is an increase of more than 1,600 times. Yet the underlying operating business did not expand by anything remotely comparable.

This is where market capitalisation needs to be understood properly.

Market capitalisation = share price × shares outstanding.

A dramatic increase in the number of shares can therefore fundamentally change the market value assigned to a company. And when a large new share base exists alongside a rising market price, the resulting headline market capitalisation can become enormous.

The critical analytical question is therefore not simply: "How much did the stock rise?"

It is: "What happened to the number of shares, the ownership structure and the underlying business at the same time?"

That distinction matters enormously in micro-cap situations.

8. The Disconnect Between Paper Value and Operating Reality

By this point, the contrast had become stark.

On one side was a listed company with a multi-thousand-crore market valuation.

On the other was a business whose operating profile remained extremely small relative to that valuation.

There were no comparable developments in operating revenue, industrial capacity or major infrastructure execution that could independently explain a move from approximately ₹3 crore to several thousand crore in market value.

The investigation therefore becomes less about the stock chart itself and more about the machinery underneath it.

A rising share price can create the appearance of wealth.

A rising share count can create the appearance of scale.

A large balance-sheet liability can create the appearance of capital.

But none of those things, by themselves, necessarily represent new economic production.

That distinction sits at the centre of the Dhenu Buildcon case.

9. The Reveal: SEBI Pulls the Curtain Back

The next significant development came after the lock-in period attached to the preferential shares expired.

According to SEBI's interim order, the preferential allottees sold shares on August 17 and August 18, 2026.

Across those two days, the six entities collectively sold 17,444 shares for approximately ₹1.34 lakh. The sales were relatively small compared with their enormous shareholdings, but they became important in the regulator's assessment of what happened after the lock-in expired.

Then came the regulatory intervention.

On August 19, 2026, SEBI issued a 112-page interim order in the matter of Dhenu Buildcon Infra Limited. The order identified Dhenu Buildcon, the six preferential allottees and two individuals, Surendra Kumar Jain and Virendra Jain, as notices in the proceedings.

SEBI's interim findings described a network involving the purported ₹1,000-crore loan inflows, multiple fund-routing transactions and the subsequent conversion of approximately ₹840 crore into equity.

The regulator restrained the relevant parties from dealing in securities in the manner specified in the order and restricted the six preferential allottees from disposing of their DBIL shares.

The order also described concerns surrounding entities that, according to the regulator's findings, lacked genuine physical or operational substance.

This is the point where the financial reconstruction became a regulatory case.

10. The FishyFinance Lens: What Investors Should Look For

The Dhenu Buildcon case offers a useful framework for examining other micro-cap situations.

First: Watch the share count, not just the share price.

A stock chart can tell you how much the market price moved. It does not tell you how many shares existed when that price was recorded.

When a company issues billions of new shares, the relationship between price, ownership and market capitalisation changes dramatically.

Always examine changes in shares outstanding alongside the price chart.

Second: Follow the origin of large unsecured loans.

A sudden appearance of hundreds of crores in unsecured loans deserves more than a glance at the balance sheet.

Who provided the money?

Where did the money originate?

Are the lenders independently funded?

Do the transaction patterns indicate ordinary business financing or repeated movement between connected entities?

The answer can be more informative than the headline loan number itself.

Third: Examine debt-to-equity conversions carefully.

A company converting debt into equity can legitimately strengthen its balance sheet.

But the economic substance of the debt matters.

If the origin of the underlying funds is unclear, the conversion deserves deeper scrutiny.

Fourth: Look at the businesses receiving preferential shares.

Preferential allotments can be legitimate instruments for raising capital and restructuring liabilities.

But when a small listed company issues an enormous number of shares to a handful of entities, investors should examine those entities carefully.

Who owns them?

What do they do?

Where are they operating?

How are they connected to the company?

And what economic value did they actually contribute?

Fifth: Separate valuation from economic activity.

A company can have a very large market capitalisation without having a proportionately large operating business.

That does not automatically make the valuation illegitimate.

But when valuation, share issuance, debt creation and operating activity move in radically different directions, the divergence deserves investigation.

12. The Bigger Lesson: Follow the Money, Not the Story

The most interesting part of the Dhenu Buildcon case is not simply that a micro-cap became a multi-thousand-crore company.

It is how many layers had to move before that valuation became possible.

First came the borrowing capacity.

Then the purported loan inflows.

Then the rapid movement of funds.

Then the accumulation of approximately ₹1,000 crore in purported unsecured loans.

Then the conversion of approximately ₹840 crore into equity.

Then billions of new shares concentrated among six entities.

And alongside all of it, a market valuation that rose from approximately ₹3 crore to several thousand crore.

The numbers create the story. And a regulatory order attempting to explain how they became connected.

13. What We Don’t Know

There is still a boundary between what has been established in the public record and what remains subject to further investigation.

What remains subject to further scrutiny

The full economic chain behind the initial ₹25.05-crore pool remains the most important unanswered question.

The existence of multiple transaction routes does not by itself explain the ultimate beneficial ownership of every rupee at the beginning of the chain.

That is precisely why forensic reconstruction matters.

Following the money through each account can reveal more than looking at the final balance-sheet number.

Markets react loudly. Capital moves quietly. We follow the movement.
